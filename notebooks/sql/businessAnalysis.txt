================================================================================================
                    BUSINESS ANALYSIS - OLIST SQL EDA (Proje_1_SQL_EDA)
                    Business analysis, outputs and edge cases of the 10 SQL queries
================================================================================================

Scope         : the .sql files numbered 01-10 under notebooks/sql/
Data source   : data/raw/ (Olist Brazilian e-commerce dataset, 9 CSVs)
Engine        : DuckDB (same table definitions as notebooks/02_sql_queries.ipynb)
Method        : Every query was run on the real data; outputs and edge cases were
                quantified with separate verification queries.
Currency      : BRL (Brazilian Real) - not stated anywhere in the queries
Report date   : 2026-08-11

TABLE SIZES
    orders        99,441      order_items   112,650      payments      103,886
    customers     99,441      reviews        99,224      products       32,951
    sellers        3,095

DATA TIME RANGE (critical)
    First order                           : 2016-09-04
    Last order (any status)               : 2018-10-17
    Last DELIVERED order (purchase date)  : 2018-08-29   <-- 7-week gap
    2018-09 has 16 orders and 2018-10 has 4, and NONE of them is delivered.

    => 7 of the queries use the "delivered" filter. As a result, the LAST 1-2 MONTHS of
       every time series are under-observed. The final point must not be used in any
       trend interpretation.


================================================================================================
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY - 8 KEY FINDINGS
================================================================================================

1.  GROWTH HAS STOPPED. Order count went from 750 in 2017-01 to a peak of 7,289 in 2017-11,
    then moved SIDEWAYS in the 6,100-7,000 band throughout 2018. Monthly revenue over the
    last 6 months is stuck in the 0.99-1.13M BRL range. This is not maturity, it is a plateau.

2.  THE ONE-ORDER CUSTOMER PROBLEM IS STRUCTURAL. 97% (93,099) of 96,096 unique customers
    placed only 1 order. Even in the best segment (Champions) the repeat order rate is 6.39%.
    The real problem of this business: customer acquisition works, retention does not.

3.  58.8% OF THE CUSTOMER BASE IS LOST (54,871 customers, last order 180+ days ago).
    Only 19.7% are active (last 90 days).

4.  REVENUE IS CONCENTRATED IN 2 SEGMENTS. By RFM, "At-Risk High-Value" (21,966 people)
    produces 42.9% of revenue and "Champions" (15,377 people) 30.6%. 40% of customers
    = 73.5% of revenue. The "At-Risk High-Value" segment = spent a lot but drifted away
    -> the most urgent win-back target.

5.  DELIVERY DELAYS HIT RATINGS DIRECTLY. Average review score is 2.57 for late orders
    and 4.29 for on-time deliveries. 54% of late orders get 1-2 stars (9.2% for on-time
    deliveries). Delay = customer-loss chain.

6.  AN OPERATIONAL CRISIS PERIOD WAS IDENTIFIED. Late rate was 21.4% in 2018-03, 16.0% in
    2018-02 and 14.3% in 2017-11 (Black Friday). Recovery follows: average delivery time
    fell from 19.6 days to 7.7 days. Capacity planning breaks during campaign periods.

7.  GEOGRAPHIC PARADOX. SP has 42% of customers and most of the revenue, but the LOWEST
    revenue per customer of any state (147.37 BRL). The highest revenue per customer is in
    PB (273.48), AC (257.71), RO (246.65) - which are also the states with the slowest
    delivery (RR 29.3 days, AP 27.2 days, AM 26.4 days; national average ~12 days). This
    revenue is inflated by freight cost; since the queries don't separate freight,
    PROFITABILITY IS UNKNOWN.

8.  PAYMENTS DEPEND ON CREDIT CARDS. 73.9% by transaction count and 78.3% by AMOUNT are
    credit card. Average installments on credit card are 3.5, but the distribution is
    bimodal (25,455 transactions paid in one go, 5,328 transactions in 10 installments) -
    the average describes no customer at all.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT ASKS : Monthly order count and average order value (delivered only).

OUTPUT (22 rows)
    month     orders    avg_value        month     orders    avg_value
    2016-10       265      165.13        2017-11     7,289      151.92  <- peak (Black Friday)
    2016-12         1       19.62        2017-12     5,513      147.18
    2017-01       750      159.63        2018-01     7,069      146.73
    2017-02     1,653      155.12        2018-02     6,555      142.64
    2017-03     2,546      153.47        2018-03     7,003      153.58
    2017-04     2,303      160.49        2018-04     6,798      160.31
    2017-05     3,546      149.74        2018-05     6,749      161.22
    2017-06     3,135      147.53        2018-06     6,099      159.38
    2017-07     3,872      136.58        2018-07     6,159      161.37
    2017-08     4,193      147.05        2018-08     6,351      150.79  <- under-observed
    2017-09     4,150      160.41
    2017-10     4,478      159.89

BUSINESS READING
    - Strong growth throughout 2017 (750 -> 7,289), a full plateau in 2018 (6,100-7,100 band).
      No month in 2018 exceeded the 2017-11 peak.
    - Basket size stayed in the 136-165 BRL band for 22 months; even though it is not
      inflation-adjusted, it DECLINED IN REAL TERMS. Growth came from volume, not the basket;
      when volume stopped, revenue stopped.
    - The query has NO revenue column. When we add it: last 6 months 1.12M / 1.13M / 1.01M /
      1.01M / 1.03M / 0.99M BRL -> flat line, slightly down.

EDGE CASES
  [E01-1] CRITICAL - Payment fan-out distorts avg_order_value.
          An order can have more than one row in the payments table (installment/voucher
          splitting). 2.98% of orders (2,962 orders) have more than 1 payment row; one
          order is split into as many as 29 rows.
          AVG(p.payment_value) gives the average per PAYMENT ROW, not per ORDER.
          Correct per-order average   : 159.86 BRL
          Value produced by the query : 153.07 BRL   -> 4.25% UNDER-REPORTING
          Fix: SUM(payment_value) per order_id first, then AVG.
          Order COUNT is correct because COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) is used - good.

  [E01-2] The last point is unreliable. 2018-08 data is partial (orders bought within the
          month but not yet delivered are missing from the series). 2018-09 (16 orders) and
          2018-10 (4 orders) don't appear at all because none of them is delivered.
          Reading the last month as "started to decline" on the chart would be wrong.

  [E01-3] 2016-12 = 1 order, avg. 19.62 BRL. A single-observation month creates a striking
          dip on the chart but is statistically meaningless. The 4 orders in 2016-09 are
          MISSING from the series entirely (see below).

  [E01-4] One order (bfbd0f9bdef84302105ad712db648a6c, 2016-09-15, delivered) has NO MATCH
          in the payments table. Because of the INNER JOIN it is silently dropped. This
          single row is the reason for the 1-person difference between Q03 (93,358
          customers) and Q10 (93,357 customers).

  [E01-5] The delivered filter excludes 2,963 orders (2.98%). Canceled/failed orders are a
          demand signal; if this is presented as a "demand trend", a separate series is needed.

  [E01-6] The time axis is order_purchase_timestamp (order date). If the accounting/revenue
          definition were the delivery or invoice date, the monthly distribution would shift.
          The definition is not written in the report.

  [E01-7] strftime(timestamp,...) is DuckDB-specific. It works in SQLite but not in
          PostgreSQL (to_char is needed). Not portable.

BUSINESS READING
    - Almost all customers at the top of the list are ONE-ORDER customers. This is not a
      "most valuable customers" list, it is a "largest single basket" list. It cannot be
      used directly for CRM/loyalty targeting.

    - The right reading from an action standpoint: high-value single baskets may be B2B /
      corporate purchases or high-priced single products. They should be examined as a
      separate segment.

  [E02-1] Grouping by customer_unique_id is the RIGHT choice. The customers table has
          99,441 rows / 99,441 customer_ids but only 96,096 unique customers. Grouping by
          customer_id would split repeat customers. This query gets that right.

  [E02-2] CRITICAL - "Split orders" are not detected. Customer #2's 2 orders:
              a96610ab...  2017-04-01 15:58:40   4,950.34 BRL
              8ea7aebc...  2017-04-01 15:58:41   2,621.29 BRL
          1 SECOND apart. This is one basket split by the system; counting it as
          "2 orders" inflates this query as well as the frequency/retention metrics (Q04,
          Q10). Orders within the same day/minute need to be merged.

  [E02-3] avg_order_value is again an average per payment row. It does NOT MATCH
          total_spend / total_orders: the two values differ for 3 of the top 100 customers.
          Small, but an inconsistent column.

  [E02-4] LIMIT 100, no OFFSET/parameter. No share of total revenue, cumulative percentage
          or Pareto column -> the question "how important is the top 100?" goes unanswered.

  [E02-5] The first_order / last_order columns give the SAME value for 97% of customers
          (single order). They produce no tenure/lifetime signal and bloat the table.

  [E02-6] No outlier handling. The single 13,664 BRL order also feeds the monetary
          quantiles in Q10 and enters the "Champions" segment as a one-time buyer.

  [E02-7] 39 unique customers appear in more than one state (same customer_unique_id,
          different customer_state). Double-counting risk in the geographic breakdown (Q09).

  [E02-8] delivered filter: large canceled baskets are not in the list. A fraud/cancellation
          analysis needs a separate view.

03_active_inactive_customers.sql
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT ASKS : Segments customers as active / inactive / lost by their last order date.

OUTPUT
    segment            customer_count   percent
    lost                      54,871    58.8
    inactive_90_180d          20,097    21.5
    active_90d                18,390    19.7
    TOTAL                     93,358

BUSINESS READING
    - 58.8% of the customer base is dead. The active customer rate is 19.7%, and even this
      "active" definition mostly means "placed their FIRST and ONLY order in the last 90 days".
    - The inactive group (20,097) is the most reasonable target for a reactivation
      campaign: not yet fully disengaged, contact consent still fresh.
    - This table alone is misleading: the size of the lost group is a result of the
      business's age and its one-order structure. Without a cohort reading (Q04) it does
      not produce action.

EDGE CASES
  [E03-1] CRITICAL - Threshold dates are HARDCODED ('2018-06-01', '2018-03-01').
          When the data is refreshed, the query SILENTLY produces wrong results; it raises
          no error, it just shows everyone as "lost". The reference date should be derived
          from the data:
              (SELECT MAX(order_purchase_timestamp) FROM orders)
          Also, Q10 uses a different reference date ('2018-09-01'). The two queries define
          "today" differently in the same report -> the segments don't match each other.

  [E03-2] The label and the math don't match. '2018-03-01' to '2018-06-01' is 92 days;
          relative to the 2018-09-01 reference, 2018-03-01 = 184 days earlier. So
          "inactive_90_180d" is actually 92-184 days. Small, but the reported label is wrong.

  [E03-3] A TIMESTAMP column is compared with a STRING (last_order_date >= '2018-06-01').
          It works correctly in DuckDB via implicit cast, but depends on the ISO-8601
          format and may silently give wrong results in other engines (or a different
          locale). An explicit DATE '2018-06-01' should be written.

  [E03-4] Because of the delivered filter, customers whose only order was canceled/
          unavailable are NOT IN the segmentation AT ALL - they aren't even counted as
          "lost". Yet a customer who never returns after a cancellation is the clearest
          churn case. 2,963 non-delivered orders become invisible this way.

  [E03-5] Population inconsistency: this query counts 93,358 customers, Q10 counts 93,357.
          Difference = the single order without a payment row in [E01-4]. Publishing two
          different "total customers" figures in the same report erodes trust.

  [E03-6] The "active" definition is not behavioral. In a base where 97% are one-order
          customers, recency measures "when they joined", not loyalty. The metric name
          creates the wrong expectation.

  [E03-7] There is a single lost threshold (ELSE). A customer who left 180 days ago and one
          who left 700 days ago are in the same bucket; win-back prioritization is impossible.

BUSINESS READING
    - Retention does not exceed 5% in any cohort. The business effectively runs a
      ONE-TIME PURCHASE model. This also explains why the F dimension in Q10 is unusable.
    - The healthiest cohorts are H1 2017 (3.4-4.3%). The decline that follows is largely
      a measurement artifact (see below) - but the gap between 2017-01 (4.3%) and 2017-10
      (2.5%) is not entirely an artifact even though they don't have the same observation
      window: earlier cohorts are better.
    - Action: retention needs to be tracked not with the cohort table but with a
      FIXED-WINDOW metric (e.g. "2nd order within 90 days after the first order").

EDGE CASES
  [E04-1] CRITICAL - RIGHT CENSORING. Cohorts don't have equal observation time. The
          2017-01 cohort had 21 months to buy again, while the 2018-08 cohort had almost
          no chance (the data ends in 2018-10).
          The "steadily falling retention" trend in the table is largely THE RESULT OF
          THIS, not a real performance decline. This table must NOT BE PRESENTED to
          management as is.
          Fix: a fixed window per cohort (M+1, M+3, etc.) or a minimum maturity filter.

  [E04-2] CRITICAL - NO order_status filter. Unlike the other 7 queries, all canceled,
          unavailable and shipped orders are used both in cohort assignment and in the
          repeat count (2,963 orders). A canceled order counts as "customer acquired".
          Population inconsistency across queries.

  [E04-3] Repeat orders within the same month are NOT COUNTED (condition:
          a.order_month > f.first_month). A customer who buys again 3 days after their
          first order appears as "did not repeat". Same-day split orders ([E02-2]) are also
          ignored here. Retention is systematically UNDER-reported.

  [E04-4] Micro-cohorts are not filtered: the 2016-12 cohort has 1 person and shows 100%
          retention. The same goes for 2016-09 (4), 2018-09 (5), 2018-10 (1). A 100% bar
          wrecks the whole scale of a chart. A threshold like HAVING cohort_size >= 50 is
          a must.

  [E04-5] This is NOT A COHORT MATRIX. It only gives the binary "ever bought again"
          outcome; there are no M+1, M+2, M+3 breakdowns, so you can't see when churn
          happens. A retention curve cannot be drawn.

  [E04-6] Months are compared as strings ('2018-01' > '2017-12'). Lexicographic ordering
          works in the '%Y-%m' format, but silently breaks if the format changes (e.g.
          '%m-%Y'). Comparing with a date type is safer.

  [E04-7] Performance/correctness: the LEFT JOIN between first_order and customer_activity
          multiplies rows per customer (98k+ rows), then collapses them with COUNT(DISTINCT
          CASE...). The result is correct but expensive and hard to read; also, the LEFT
          JOIN is in practice an INNER join (every customer has at least 1 activity) -
          it sends a misleading signal.

05_return_anomaly.sql
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT ASKS : The file name says "return anomaly"; the query actually computes the
               CANCELLATION RATE PER CATEGORY and the deviation from the overall average
               (min 50 orders, top 20).

OUTPUT (20 rows - first 10)
    category                          orders  canceled  cancel%  deviation
    dvds_blu_ray                           59      2    3.39  +2.87
    telefonia_fixa                        217      3    1.38  +0.86
    livros_interesse_geral                512      7    1.37  +0.85
    eletrodomesticos_2                    234      3    1.28  +0.76
    instrumentos_musicais                 628      8    1.27  +0.75
    eletroportateis                       630      8    1.27  +0.75
    construcao_ferramentas_seguranca      167      2    1.20  +0.68
    construcao_ferramentas_jardim         194      2    1.03  +0.51
    NULL  (!)                           1,451     14    0.96  +0.44
    fashion_roupa_masculina               112      1    0.89  +0.37
    overall_avg = 0.519322 (unrounded)

BUSINESS READING
    - The apparent picture: cancellation rates range from 0.6-3.4% by category, the overall
      level is low. At first glance one would say "we don't have a cancellation problem".
    - THIS IS NOT THE REAL PICTURE. Because of [E05-1] below, most of the unfulfilled
      orders never enter this analysis. The output of this query CANNOT currently be used
      as a risk signal.
    - The only meaningful structural finding: cancellation is a rare event (true overall
      rate 0.63%) and category differences stay below small-sample noise.

EDGE CASES
  [E05-1] MOST CRITICAL FINDING - Silent undercount / survivorship bias.
          The query builds the products -> order_items -> orders chain with INNER JOINs.
          But most failed orders have NO order_items RECORD:
              unavailable : only   6 of 609 orders are in order_items  (1.0%)
              canceled    :      461 of 625 orders are in order_items  (73.8%)
          So 767 of 1,234 problem orders are INVISIBLE in this analysis. The
          cancellation/non-fulfillment rate is structurally UNDER-reported and the worst
          cases (unavailable) disappear completely.

  [E05-2] The 'unavailable' status is not included in the numerator at all. From the
          customer's point of view, "out of stock, your order could not be fulfilled" is
          the clearest failure; the query only counts 'canceled'.

  [E05-3] The file name doesn't match the content. The dataset has NO RETURN (return/refund)
          data. The name "return_anomaly" creates the wrong expectation for the reader. The
          only signal usable as a return proxy is reviews.review_score / the review text.

  [E05-4] Fan-out: multi-category orders are counted twice. 727 orders contain products from
          more than one category. The sum of per-category order counts is 99,470; the real
          order count is 98,666. The percentages are on a "category-order" base, not an
          "order" base; the table doesn't say so.

  [E05-5] The NULL category comes out as an ANALYSIS ROW (1,451 orders, 610 products).
          A "NaN" row appears in the output. Either COALESCE(...,'unknown') or an explicit
          filter is needed; in its current state it is not presentation-ready.

  [E05-6] Statistical invalidity. The HAVING >= 50 threshold is too low. dvds_blu_ray at
          the top means 2 CANCELLATIONS in 59 orders; a single cancellation moves the rate
          by 1.7%. The "top 20 anomalies" list is effectively a "categories with the
          smallest samples" list. No confidence interval / proportion test (e.g. binomial).

  [E05-7] overall_avg uses the WRONG BASE. AVG(cancel_pct) OVER () = the unweighted average
          of category rates (0.5193), computed AFTER the HAVING (i.e. only over categories
          with 50+ orders). The true overall cancellation rate is 0.63%.
          The "deviation" column is measured against the wrong reference. A weighted rate
          should be used.

  [E05-8] The overall_avg column is not ROUNDed -> a noisy column like 0.519322 in the
          output. All other columns are rounded; inconsistent.

  [E05-9] The ORDER BY cancel_pct DESC + LIMIT 20 combination, together with [E05-6],
          systematically pushes small categories to the top. Ordering together with an
          absolute cancellation COUNT column (impact-based) would be more correct.

    month     orders   late     late%    avg.days    month    orders   late     late%    avg.days

    2017-04     2,303      181       7.9     15.0     2018-05    6,749      556      8.2     11.3
    2017-05     3,545      128       3.6     11.4     2018-06    6,096       83      1.4      9.2
    2017-06     3,135      121       3.9     12.0     2018-07    6,156      276      4.5      8.9

    2017-09     4,150      216       5.2     11.7     TOTAL LATE: 7,826 orders
    2017-10     4,478      237       5.3     11.7

BUSINESS READING (the most actionable part of the report)
    - Three crisis periods stand out: 2017-11 (14.3% - Black Friday), 2018-02 (16.0%) and
      2018-03 (21.4% - 1 in every 5 orders late). In 2018-03, 1,496 customers experienced
      a delivery where the promise was broken.
    - Delivery TIME shows steady improvement: ~12-15 days on average in 2017,
      8.9 / 7.7 days in 2018-07/08. Operations are getting faster.
    - But the late RATE and the delivery time don't move together (2018-08: time 7.7 days,
      the best; late rate 10.4%, bad). This shows that promised dates
      (estimated_delivery_date) are calculated too optimistically. The problem is not
      transport speed but the PROMISE-SETTING policy.
    - Business impact (additional analysis): avg. review 2.57 for late orders / 4.29 for
      on-time. 54% of late orders get 1-2 stars (9.2% for on-time). Roughly 4,200 of the
      7,826 late orders turn directly into negative reviews.

EDGE CASES
  [E06-1] CRITICAL - The late definition misuses the time component.
          ALL of order_estimated_delivery_date (99,441 rows) is at 00:00:00.
          order_delivered_customer_date, however, contains the real time. So an order
          delivered at 14:00 within the promised DAY is counted as "late".
          1,292 (16.5%) of the 7,826 "late" orders were delivered ON THE SAME CALENDAR DAY.
          True lateness is ~6,534 orders. All monthly late rates are INFLATED by about 16%.
          Fix: DATE_TRUNC('day', order_delivered_customer_date) > order_estimated_delivery_date

  [E06-2] The SIZE of the delay is not measured. A 1-day delay and a 30-day delay count the
          same. Customer impact and compensation cost are completely different.
          An AVG(DATEDIFF('day', estimated, delivered)) column should be added.

  [E06-3] Undelivered orders are NOT in the analysis. The worst customer experience
          (1,107 shipped + 609 unavailable + 625 canceled) doesn't show up in the "delay"
          metric. The metric is computed only over orders that eventually succeeded
          -> optimistic.

  [E06-4] 2018-08 CENSORING. Orders bought within the month but not yet delivered are
          missing from the series; only FAST deliveries show up. This both makes
          avg_delivery_days (7.7) look artificially good and distorts the late rate.
          The statement "delivery time dropped to 7.7 days" is indefensible as is.

  [E06-5] 8 orders have order_status='delivered' yet order_delivered_customer_date is NULL.
          They are correctly excluded by the WHERE, but this data-quality issue shows the
          'delivered' status can't be fully trusted (these 8 orders are counted in Q01 but
          not in Q06 -> small count differences between the two queries come from here;
          e.g. 2017-05: 3,546 vs 3,545).

  [E06-6] The 2016-09 row: 1 order, 100% late, 55 days. It hijacks the entire y axis on a
          chart. No minimum order threshold.

  [E06-7] avg_delivery_days measures purchase -> delivery; approval, preparation and
          shipping times are not separated. It is unknown at which stage the improvement
          happened (the order_approved_at and order_delivered_carrier_date columns are

PART 2 - CROSS-CUTTING (CROSS-QUERY) EDGE CASE LOG
================================================================================================

[X-1] GRAIN INCONSISTENCY - the biggest source of errors
      The tables have different grains: orders (1 row/order), order_items (1 row/item),
      payments (1 row/payment transaction), reviews (1 row/review, 2 for 547 orders).
      When these tables are JOINed, rows multiply and every metric that takes an AVERAGE
      is distorted.
      Affected: Q01 (AOV 4.25% low), Q02 (avg. order value), Q07 (avg. rating),
      Q08 (all metrics).
      SUM and COUNT(DISTINCT) are robust to fan-out; AVG IS NOT. Rule: in multi-table
      queries, aggregate at the right grain first (CTE), then take the average.

[X-2] order_status FILTER INCONSISTENCY
      delivered filter : 01, 02, 03, 06, 07, 09, 10
      no filter        : 04, 05, 08
      Result: populations don't match across queries (e.g. total payments 16.0M BRL,
      delivered revenue 15.42M BRL). Using different universes within the same report is
      indefensible in a presentation. A single filter policy (and its rationale) must be set.

[X-3] TIME CUTOFF / CENSORING
      Purchase data runs to 2018-10-17, delivered orders to 2018-08-29.
      The last 1-2 points of every time series (Q01, Q04, Q06) are under-observed.
      The entire cohort table (Q04) is under right censoring.
      RULE: the last month must not be interpreted on any chart; cohort tables must be
      converted to a fixed window.

[X-4] HARDCODED DATES, DIFFERENT DEFINITIONS OF "TODAY"
      Q03: '2018-06-01' / '2018-03-01'     Q10: '2018-09-01'
      Neither is derived from the data. When the data is refreshed they silently give
      wrong results (they raise no error - the most dangerous type of bug). They should
      be tied to a single parameter.

[X-5] NO SMALL-SAMPLE CONTROL
      Q04 (1-person cohort, 100% retention), Q05 (2 cancellations in 59 orders = "top
      anomaly"), Q06 (100% late in a 1-order month), Q09 (a state with 40 customers).
      No query has a minimum n threshold or a confidence interval. The top of every ranking
      is systematically filled with NOISE.

[X-6] INVISIBILITY OF FAILED ORDERS
      1,107 shipped + 625 canceled + 609 unavailable + 314 invoiced + 301 processing
      = 2,963 orders (2.98%). The delivered filters drop them; in Q05, 99% of unavailable
      orders have no order_items record, so they are structurally invisible. The WORST
      side of the customer experience is not measured in any query.

[X-7] UNUSED DATA SOURCES
      olist_geolocation_dataset.csv        -> never loaded (no city/zip/coordinate analysis)
      product_category_name_translation.csv-> never loaded (Q05 output is in Portuguese,
                                              "dvds_blu_ray", "eletroportateis", etc.)
      Unused columns as well: order_approved_at, order_delivered_carrier_date
      (delivery-stage analysis), review_comment_message (return/complaint proxy),
      product_weight_g / dimensions (freight cost model).

[X-8] AMBIGUOUS REVENUE DEFINITION
      payments.payment_value and order_items.(price+freight_value) don't match for 380
      orders (0.39%). Q01/02/09/10 use payment_value, Q07 uses price (excluding freight).
      "Revenue" must be fixed to a single definition across the report.

[X-9] PORTABILITY / ENGINE DEPENDENCE
      The uses of strftime, DATEDIFF, CUME_DIST, ::INT, CEIL are DuckDB-specific.
      The queries can't be moved to PostgreSQL or SQLite as is. If they go to production,
      a target engine must be chosen.

[X-10] NOTEBOOK ENCODING RISK
      In notebooks/02_sql_queries.ipynb the queries are read with open(...); encoding='utf-8'
      is given ONLY for 10_rfm_segmentation.sql. On Windows the default encoding is
      cp1254; any .sql file containing Turkish characters raises UnicodeDecodeError or is
      read corrupted (Q10 was already fixed for exactly this reason).
      encoding='utf-8' should be standardized in ALL cells.

[X-11] SPLIT ORDERS
      The same customer's orders created seconds apart are counted separately ([E02-2]).
      This inflates the frequency (Q02, Q10), retention (Q04) and order count (Q01)
      metrics. A rule is needed to merge orders within the same day.

[X-12] CURRENCY AND INFLATION
      No query states the currency (BRL). No adjustment was made for Brazilian inflation
      between 2016-2018; the statement "basket size stayed flat" is nominal - in real
      terms it is a DECLINE.

[X-13] OUTPUTS ARE NOT PERSISTED
      The reports/ and src/ folders are EMPTY. The queries only run inside the notebook,
      results are not written to disk; no reproducibility and no version tracking.
